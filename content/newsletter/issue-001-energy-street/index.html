Tank sloshing CFD study with open source software OpenFOAM

The sloshing computational fluid dynamics (CFD) study reproduced the tank's small-amplitude first-mode frequency within about 0.30 % of linear theory across the tested fill levels. Sloshing matters in tanks on floating vessels. Near resonance, the liquid runs up the tank walls and strikes them. A partly filled tank tuned near a vessel's roll period can also act as an anti-roll damper. Read the study.

The analysis results for the two-dimensional tank are compared to the available public literature to sense-check/benchmark the results. Parametric analysis for other tank dimensions, fill levels and forcing can be constructed and rerun by extending the same scripts. Impact-pressure validation is out of scope.

This study is extended to assess a U-tube style anti-roll arrangement: two connected tanks with invented geometry. This study is an analytical screening model calibrated on CFD assessment. Based on the benchmarked single tank analysis, analysis is extended to build the connected model.

Read the dual-tank study

A separate screening estimate, with a public U-tube reference tank, gives the roll of a generic hull. The result depends mainly on hull damping and on how large the tank's water moment is compared with the hull's own roll stiffness. That ratio is called alpha: the tank's quasi-static free-surface moment stiffness divided by the hull's roll restoring. At an 18 s hull roll period, alpha 0.05 and conduit area 6.8 m², peak roll reduction is 46.1 % at 2 % hull damping. At 5 % hull damping it is 23.3 %. Both cases lie below the 20 s band of the tank fit, and no real-vessel test backs them.

One place to work

We now use AI assistants for many tasks: in a chat window or an app, on a phone, and on personal and work computers. AI assistants use language models that do not always give the same answer. We are running into the familiar territory of "the answer can change from one run to the next, and with the wording of the question."

When we dealt with IT and programmers, many a times, we faced a related frustration: "oh.. but it works on my machine." This answer was so familiar for administrative tasks such as opening a Microsoft word file or crash). Equally, the same experience when we ran a script not able to run let alone getting same result. We now meet a similar problem in everyday AI use: one person gets a result that another cannot repeat.

One of the solutions is to keep each job in one folder, with its files, its tools, its prompts and its record of changes. Let your AI assistant work through this created set environment. In our own workflow, we aim to retain the prompt, inputs and available assistant record for each task. To check a result, or run it again, you need its prompt, its inputs and that record. So the home for this data matters.

The article shows how this grows from one engineer to a team and then to the company. In our own practice, building this habit takes repeated use; the effort and benefits vary by task. Treating AI assistants as loyal team members who need help will get us a long way. Similar to training your employee team, training this single work storage place will make immense difference.
