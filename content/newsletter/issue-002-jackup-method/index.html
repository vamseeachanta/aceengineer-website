Jack-up site-specific analysis

Prepared: Analyst Deckhand (AI assistant) · Checked: Editor Deckhand · Approved: Vamsee Achanta · 3 November 2026

On 24 October 2026, a jack-up drilling unit sank offshore Gabon. Of the 115 people on board, 110 were accounted for and brought ashore [1, 2]. Press reports of the dead and missing differ and await official confirmation [1, 2]. Our thoughts are with everyone affected, and with their families and colleagues.

Gabon's Ministry of Petroleum and Gas has ordered a technical investigation [1, 2]. The rig owner, Borr Drilling, said on 25 October 2026 that "preliminary information indicates that the rig experienced a punch through event during preload activities" [18]. The investigation's findings are awaited. Apart from the rig owner's statement, nothing in this piece bears on the cause of the loss. This piece is to demonstrate and progress complex engineering methods and workflows. We would like to use this opportunity to develop reusable engineering for the jack-up and the liftboat, in a bid to learn and also to help with future site studies for similar structures.

2 Jack-ups, liftboats and wind installation jack-ups all stand on legs

A drilling contractor's annual report counts 494 jack-ups in the global fleet at the end of 2025 [9]. On 1 August 2022, 77 US-flagged liftboats were in active service [10]. We found no public world count of liftboats or of wind installation jack-ups.

The three types of unit work in the same way:

  • A jack-up is a mobile unit with a floating hull and legs that it lowers to the seabed. It then raises the hull clear of the sea.
  • A liftboat is an offshore supply vessel with movable legs capable of raising its hull above the surface of the sea [7]. It has three or four legs and its own propulsion, and it serves oil platforms and wind turbines [10].
  • Wind installation jack-ups, or wind turbine installation vessels, are "self-propelled heavy-lift jack-up vessels designed for the installation of offshore wind turbines" [11].
  • All three preload their legs to test the soil before they raise the hull to its working height [4; 12; 13, p. 77].

3 Serious jack-up and liftboat accidents, 1999 to 2022: 35 events in three public sources

Liftboats and wind installation jack-ups have an accident record like that of drilling jack-ups. A 2019 review of self-elevating platform incidents finds liftboat incidents "identical to Jack-up incidents" [12]. For wind installation jack-ups, the 2010 MMS report states: "Much can be anticipated about incidents and accidents by looking at the track record of jack-ups in the North Sea and liftboats in Gulf of Mexico" [13].

Figure 2 shows all 35 serious accidents in three public sources, by year and initiating hazard [10, 13 to 17]. The MMS report lists 14 accidents of jack-up construction vessels (construction jack-ups below), mostly at wind farms, from 1999 to 2008. The 2021 review lists 17 of the worst drilling jack-up accidents from 2012 to mid-2021. Four NTSB reports cover one liftboat each.

By our count of Table 2, 17 events are on drilling jack-ups, 13 on construction jack-ups and 5 on liftboats. By our hazard grouping (Note 1), 4 start with storm or weather, 11 with punch-through or foundation, and 4 with leg, jacking or structural failure. Another 4 happen in transit or tow, and 12 are other or not stated.

Note 1: the hazard groups are ours. An event under tow or in transport is transit or tow. Other events follow the source's cause code or wording; where the source states no hazard, the event is other or not stated.

Each source covers its own scope and period, so the chart dates these events; the rates below come from the 2021 review. Over a longer span, that review counts 90 liftboat losses, with 56 deaths [14]. It puts the liftboat loss rate at 2 to 3 times that of drilling jack-ups [14]. On jack-ups, deaths per operating rig-year fell from 0.07 to 0.007 over 61 years of records [14].

5 What a site-specific assessment covers

Before a jack-up works at a new location, a site-specific assessment checks the unit at that location [3, 4]. The assessment covers:

  • The foundation: how the seabed at that location carries the footings at the foot of each leg (spudcans). SNAME 5-5A lists foundation hazards that include punch-through, settlement or bearing failure, sliding, scour and seafloor instability [4, Table 3.1]. In a punch-through, a leg breaks through a strong soil layer into weaker soil below and drops quickly.
  • The structure: the strength of the legs, the hull and the holding system, the machinery that holds the hull at its height on the legs.
  • Overturning and sliding: whether the whole unit stays upright and in place in the assessment storm, the extreme storm that the unit is checked against at that site.
  • The air gap: the clearance between the hull and the highest wave crest.
  • The moves: the stability of the unit while it floats, under tow or under its own power. For a jack-up, this follows the stability chapter of the IMO code for mobile offshore drilling units (MODUs) [6]; for a liftboat, the US Coast Guard rules [8].

In US federal waters, Title 30 of the US Code of Federal Regulations (30 CFR) covers the use of a MODU in section 250.713 [5]. Under 250.713(a), the operator must show the unit's capability at the proposed location, including the minimum air gap. Under 250.713(b), the operator must also show that the site-specific soil and oceanographic conditions can support a bottom-founded unit, one that stands on the seabed [5].

For a liftboat, we apply the foundation, structure and air gap checks as for a jack-up. Its overturning check must also meet the US Coast Guard's special rules for liftboats, sections 174.240 to 174.260 of Title 46 of the CFR [8]. For a liftboat in restricted service, 46 CFR 174.255(c) sets on-bottom stability: a continuous downward force on each footing under waves, currents and 70-knot winds in normal operation, and 100-knot winds in a severe storm [8]. The same sections set its stability afloat and its freeboard [8].

7 How the methods serve future site studies

  • Each site study starts from the same data list (Table 3). Each input is entered with its source and date, so a later study can see what was measured and what was assumed.
  • Jack-ups and liftboats both raise their hulls on legs, so the checks of section 5 are set out for both; section 5 gives the checks that carry over to a liftboat and the US Coast Guard rules that add to them.
  • The accident record of section 3 is kept beside the data list. Each new site study can then be read against the events seen on similar units.
  • The inputs and results of each site study are kept with its prompt, for the next study of a similar structure.

Try it out: for your next jack-up or liftboat move, list each input to the site assessment with its source and date.

Reusable prompts

Set out a site-specific assessment of a jack-up or liftboat at a new location: list the site and unit data it needs (Table 3), set out each check of section 5 with the edition and clause of each code it uses, and mark every input with its source. Then count the jack-ups and liftboats in service from public operator filings and investigation reports. Chart past serious accidents of all kinds by year, initiating hazard and unit type, and on a world map, each with its source. Name no cause for any event still under investigation.

Data: the public data sets for each input, such as the GEBCO grid, the NOAA National Data Buoy Center records and ERA5, are named in Table 3.
