Gulf of Mexico well permits: a public US data file

Prepared: Analyst Deckhand · Checked: Editor Deckhand · Approved: Vamsee Achanta · 3 November 2026

Public data shows how new-well permits in US federal waters of the Gulf of America (formerly the Gulf of Mexico) have changed since 2005. Each year now has far fewer permits than in the mid-2000s. Most permits once named a jack-up rig; since 2015, most name a drillship (Figure 1). Since 2015, most permits are also for wells in 500 m of water or more (Figure 2). This page counts one public file, the Application for Permit to Drill raw data of the US Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE), by rig type and water depth. It sets out what the file holds and the steps to repeat the counts, as a starting point for readers who want to explore the data further.

The BSEE Data Center is the bureau's public data service, and the Application for Permit to Drill file is one of its raw data files. The file is listed as updated daily. The same raw data page also lists files on boreholes, platform structures, pipelines, production and offshore statistics by water depth, for readers who want to look further.

Well permits by rig type and water depth

The permit file holds each application for a permit to drill in US federal waters. Each record gives the permit type, the requested spud date (the date drilling is to start), the water depth, the rig name and the rig type. Here each well is counted once, at its first New Well permit in the Gulf of Mexico region. It is counted in the year of its requested spud date, with the rig type that permit names. From 2005 to 2026, 2,895 wells are counted this way. The rule leaves out each well's later permits, and the 10 wells whose first New Well permit gives no requested spud date. The year 2026 runs only to the file of 2 November 2026; later files can differ, because BSEE updates the files daily.

The counting script names six rig types: jack-up, drillship, semisubmersible, DP semisubmersible (dynamically positioned, held on station by its thrusters), platform rig and submersible. Barge, barge rig and well intervention vessel (19 permits) are grouped as other. Figure 1 shows the new-well permits each year by rig type.

Every permit counted records a water depth. The file gives it in feet; this page gives it in metres (1 ft = 0.3048 m). Figure 2 shows the new-well permits each year in four water-depth bands: under 100 m, 100 to 500 m, 500 to 1,500 m, and 1,500 m and deeper. A depth on a band edge is counted in the deeper band.

Our hypothesis: with few new-well permits now naming a jack-up (Figure 1), jack-up work in US federal Gulf of Mexico waters will turn further from new wells to plugging and abandonment.

Our hypothesis: with most new-well permits since 2015 in 500 m of water or more (Figure 2), new-well engineering in these waters will centre further on drilling, risers and subsea equipment for deep water.

Each count here takes the rig type named in a well's first permit. This page leaves open how often a different type of rig drilled the well.
