The model we use matters

The Deckhand Chronicles is AceEngineer's newsletter. We help build AI into everyday engineering work. By Vamsee Achanta, P.E., AceEngineer's founder; "my experience" below is his.

Main Street · AI and engineering work. Main Street covers how engineering work is changing.

What we did

Benjamin Graham's Mr. Market names a stock price every day, and the investor is free to take it or leave it [4]. In our work, each new AI model has been an offer of that kind every other week that is too good to turn down. We work with AI models every day, and we keep improving everything around them. Many things play a role; we show them on two examples, a technical calculation and project management, from our own work, using below aspects:

  • The model itself.
  • The instructions we give, curated over time.
  • The data and context it gets: project files, earlier work and our rules.
  • The tools it can run, and the checks on its answers.
  • How we nudge and hand-hold it.

This is the first article of our series on working with AI models.

Why it matters

  • For a simple problem, a model from any provider may do the work.
  • As the problem grows more complex, the model, the context, and the nudging and hand-holding make a large difference.
  • The chosen model can make a bigger difference as it makes a better decision at every turn, and the cumulative good turns make an excellent result.
  • Reusable prompt to check the examples and test each new model on your own problems.

What is inside

The model you use

Our hypothesis: AI models improve in steps. A new model can solve problems that the model before it could not.

In my experience, an older model often does not say "I cannot solve this". It gives an answer that looks correct.

One step from our own records: the same change to our engineering knowledge base, reviewed at the same commit by two generations of one model. The earlier model, Claude Fable 5.1, marked one finding to fix before merge. The next generation, Claude Fable 5.2, marked five, including that one (Figure 1). After this review and one other, we moved our technical reviewers to the newer model.

Simple problems and complex ones

In our experience, the model may matter less for simpler problems. As complexity increases, the model we choose and the context we provide matter more. The context is what the model is given with the task: the inputs and their units, the design code and edition, the checks to apply and a worked example.

In my experience, a default set-up, such as a Sonnet model in GitHub Copilot, handles a unit conversion, a format change or a short script well. A frontier model, such as Claude Fable, earns its place when the work runs over many steps: reading a project, planning, calculating, checking and fixing.

No single provider wins every task. In the examples below, each of the two providers we use, Claude (Anthropic) [2] and Codex (OpenAI) [3], is ahead on one kind of work. That is why we test each new model on our own problems, not on its reputation.

A calculation with a known answer

A model can give a confident answer that is wrong, and a simple check may not find it. The wrong answer has the right units, a clean formula and a confident tone. A check written by the same model can repeat the same mistake, so it passes. Only a check against a known answer, worked from the source, finds it.

The problem: the berthing energy that a fender must absorb when a ship comes alongside. We were adding the method of UFC 4-152-01 [1], a public US design code for piers and wharves, to our open-source fender design module. The module is under review and is not yet merged. Part of the energy is the water that moves with the hull, the added mass. Its factor F depends on a coefficient Cb.

  • The code defines Cb as the berthing coefficient, the product of four factors for eccentricity, geometry, softness and berth configuration (Eq. 5-2).
  • A ship's block coefficient, a measure of hull fullness, has its own symbol in the code.
  • Both look like "Cb" in ordinary naval-architecture writing.

A Claude model wrote the module, and used the block coefficient. Its own hand-check test made the same substitution, so the test passed. A Codex model then reviewed the module as our second provider. It read the definition in the code's text, reproduced the case and asked for a change.

The case: a ship 180 m long, 32 m beam, 12 m draught, in 14 m of water, block coefficient 0.40, berthing at midships at 0.10 m/s.

Mass               M  = 0.40 x 1.025 x 180 x 32 x 12      = 28,339 t
Berthing coeff.    Cb = Ce Cg Cd Cc = 1.0 (>= 0.6)        -> F = 0.9
Added mass factor  Cm = 3.876  (with F = 0.9)
Design energy      E  = 0.5 x M x v^2 x Cm x 1.5          = 823.7 kNm

With the block coefficient in its place (0.40 < 0.6)      -> F = 0.6
                   Cm = 2.843                              -> 604.2 kNm

The first answer, 604.2 kNm, is about 27 % low. A fender chosen for it would be too small. It had every sign of a right answer: a cited code, the right units and a passing test. The Claude author then checked the code's text, agreed that the reviewer was right, fixed the module, and added a new test worked by hand from the code's own equations: 823.7 kNm. Over nine reviews of the module, Codex raised 24 findings at its two highest priority levels, and all were fixed before its first pass. One of them disagreed with a vendor manual's printed worked example.

What it shows: a second model, given the source and asked to reproduce the case, found what the first model's own check could not. The check that counts is a known answer worked from the source.

Project management, decision by decision

Project work is a long chain of small decisions: what to start, what to hold, what to leave to the owner. One wrong turn is cheap to fix. The same wrong turn, repeated every hour, is not.

Our ecosystem runs work on several computers. Each hour, a fleet manager decides what to start, what to hold, what to collect and what to leave to the owner. A Claude desk holds that role: Claude Opus 5.5 on our main workstation.

Since 15 Nov 2026, a Codex model has shadowed it. Each hour, the shadow gets a frozen copy of the same inputs and makes its own decisions. It runs in a closed sandbox: it writes files and posts nothing. On 19 Nov 2026 the shadow was GPT-6.2 Astra, the Codex model on all our computers since 17 Nov, at high reasoning effort.

How it is scored:

  • Before reading the shadow's output, a Claude scorer writes down the desk's decisions for that hour. This is the reference.
  • Each reference decision is marked agree, disagree or missed.
  • Each extra proposal of the shadow is checked by hand and marked valid or invalid.
  • Score = agreed / (agreed + disagreed + missed + invalid extras).
  • A run passes at 0.80 or more, with no critical miss. A critical miss is, for example, a step the owner had held, or a red alarm left without action.

The scores on 19 Nov 2026, 24 hourly runs (Figure 2):

  • 0.00 to 0.43, mean 0.21. No run passed.
  • Of the desk's 149 decisions, the shadow agreed on 42, disagreed on 8 and missed 99.
  • It made 58 invalid proposals. It also made 306 valid ones, which the score does not count for it.
  • Since 15 Nov, in more than 80 hourly runs on this model and the one before it, one run passed: a backtest on 16 Nov, at 0.88.

Three decisions, in plain words:

  1. Agree. Fourteen pull requests were ready to merge. In our rules, merging is the owner's step. The desk left ten in the owner's merge step, left a rule change to his choice, and kept three that depended on an unmerged one waiting. The shadow agreed, for the first time in seven runs. In the six runs before, it proposed to merge them itself.
  2. Disagree. A computer sat idle while the job feeder said it had nothing to send. The shadow proposed a regular refill. The desk traced the feeder and found three jobs reserved for a computer that was offline, where they could never be picked up. It released them and delivered two to the idle computer. A refill delivers nothing while the jobs sit reserved for the offline computer.
  3. Invalid, repeated. In its eleventh run with the same item, the shadow proposed a disk clean-up diagnosis on the main workstation that needs the owner's administrator rights, against a standing rule. In the same run it missed a client fix that the owner had held until he says "resume": a critical miss.

To read these scores fairly:

  • The reference is the Claude desk's own decisions. The test asks how often Codex makes the same call; it does not prove each desk call was the best one.
  • Many misses were events after the shadow's inputs froze, such as an owner request a few minutes later. It could not see them.
  • The shadow was sometimes ahead. One hour before the desk did, it proposed moving a job off the offline computer to an idle one.
  • Most of its invalid items were repeats. Once a standing rule named a repeat, it mostly stopped.

In my experience, this is where the model makes a bigger difference: it makes a better decision at every turn, and the cumulative good turns make an excellent result. For project management, the Claude desk keeps the role.

Keep your future problem sets

Keep your future problem sets: the problems you would most like a model to solve. They are your dreams for your work. Dream big.

When you get access to a new model, run the set on the first day. Compare each answer with the known answer, or check it the way you planned. Keep some money or spare usage ready for this test. When a dream is not realised with this model, keep it for the next one. Raise the complexity of the set step by step as each model solves more.

Keep two kinds of problem in the set:

  • Calculations with a known answer, worked from the source, like the fender case above.
  • Decisions with a known good call, recorded from your own past work, like the project management hours above.

There is always a second route: break the problem down. With more time spent splitting a problem into smaller steps, each with a known answer, today's model may solve it. The other route is to wait for the next frontier model. Balance the effort now against the work later, by what is needed now.

The set shows you, on your own work, what the new model can now do. It also shows which checks you must still do by hand.

Appendix A Method and data

The method and data behind the examples are summarised in this appendix.

  • Fender case: the UFC 4-152-01 berthing energy method [1], 5-2.1.1. The ship and berth inputs are a generic case, not a project. The hand check is worked from the code's text, not from the module; the module's test reruns it and also checks the 604.2 kNm of the superseded reading.
  • Project management: each hourly shadow run has its frozen inputs, its prompt, the reference written before the output was read, the marks and the score table. The records stay in our private repository because they name hosts and client work; the counts above are taken from them.
  • Model names are as recorded on each side. Where a record does not name the model, the article says so.

Hand check of the fender case, under review in an open pull request and not yet merged: digitalmodel on GitHub, test_methods_codes.py.

Appendix B Reusable prompts

  • Recompute the fender case by hand from the UFC text with your own assistant, once with the berthing coefficient and once with the block coefficient, and check both against the published values.
  • Then turn your own problems into a repeatable check of each model and provider: a calculation with a known answer, and a decision with a known good call from your own records.
You are checking an AI model on problems whose answers I know.
For each problem below: solve it, state every assumption and the source
clause you used, and give the result with units. Do not look up my answers.
Then I will compare your result with the known answer and mark it
agree, disagree or missed.
Problems: <paste your problem set>

Public data set: AceEngineer publishes the generic fender case inputs and the 19 Nov 2026 hourly scores (Figure 2 and Table 1) on the article page, 20 Nov 2026.
