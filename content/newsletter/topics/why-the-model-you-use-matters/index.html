The model we use matters

The Deckhand Chronicles is AceEngineer's newsletter. We help build AI into everyday engineering work. By Vamsee Achanta, P.E., AceEngineer's founder; "my experience" below is his.

Main Street · AI and engineering work. Main Street covers how engineering work is changing.

What we did

Benjamin Graham's Mr. Market names a price every day, and the investor is free to take it or leave it [5]. In our work, each new AI model has been an offer of that kind that is too good to turn down. We work with AI models every day, and we keep improving everything around them. Many things play a role; we show them on one engineering example, using below aspects:

  • The model itself.
  • The instructions we give it.
  • The data and context it gets: project files, earlier work and our rules.
  • The tools it can run, and the checks on its answers.
  • How we nudge and hand-hold it.

Why it matters

  • For a simple problem, a model from any provider may do the work.
  • As the problem grows more complex, the model, the context, and the nudging and hand-holding make a large difference.
  • A model can give a confident answer that is wrong, and a simple check may not find it.
  • Reusable prompt to check the example and test each new model on your own problems.

The model you use

Our hypothesis: AI models improve in steps. A new model can solve problems that the model before it could not.

In my experience, an older model often does not say "I cannot solve this". It gives an answer that looks correct.

Simple problems and complex ones

In our experience, the model may matter less for simpler problems. As complexity increases, the model we choose and the context we provide matter more. The context is what the model is given with the task: the inputs and their units, the design code and edition, the checks to apply and a worked example.

Frontier and behind

A frontier model is one of the most capable AI models available on a given date. A model one or two releases behind may cost less or respond faster for some workloads. Cost and speed depend on the models, workload and current pricing. Test whether the cheaper model meets your task's requirements, even for a summary, format change or first draft of a letter.

A small gain can be a step change. Suppose many of your problems sit just beyond what a model two releases behind can do. Suppose, too, that a newer model scores only 1 to 2 % higher on public benchmarks (a hypothetical figure). It could then solve a whole group of your problems that the older model got wrong. For that group, the result is not 1 to 2 % better: it is a right answer in place of a wrong one. Figure 1 shows the idea; it is not a measurement.

Engineering that a newer model knows unaided

Our hypothesis: a newer model may also take on more engineering itself, as more mathematics and physics are trained into it.

In my experience, our code once had to spell out the catenary equation for a hanging line. A frontier model now knows that equation without being given it. On this view, engineering that once had to be written out for a model becomes something it knows unaided. Figure 2 shows this direction in three stages. Where the engineer keeps the check, the work is checked against a known answer at every stage and then signed. A problem set with known answers, described below, is that check.

A worked example: a confident shortcut

This example is an illustration of the kind of error, not a test result. It is provided as a demonstration for this article.

The problem: find the first sloshing frequency of a rectangular tank, 1.0 m long, filled to 0.15 m.

The full linear-theory answer (small motions) includes the fill depth. For the first mode of a two-dimensional rectangular tank:

L = 1.0 m        tank length in the direction of sloshing
h = 0.15 m       fill depth
g = 9.81 m/s^2   gravity
k = pi / L       = 3.14 per m              (wave number of the first mode)
tanh(k h)        = tanh(0.471) = 0.439      (hyperbolic tangent; it tends to 1 for a deep fill)
w^2 = g k tanh(k h)  -> w = 3.68 rad/s     (w: angular frequency)
f = w / (2 pi)   = 0.586 Hz

A shortcut drops the tanh term. That form is for deep liquid:

w^2 = g k        -> w = 5.55 rad/s,  f = 0.884 Hz

The shortcut answer is about 51 % too high. It looks plausible: it has the right units, a clean formula and a confident tone. At a deep fill the two forms almost agree: at 0.70 m fill the difference is about 1 %. A check at a deep fill does not find the error. Only a check at the shallow fill shows it.

A frontier model can make the same mistake.

Our hypothesis: a frontier model is less likely to take this shortcut. It knows more mathematics, and it checks its own assumptions more often.

Keep your future problem sets

Keep your future problem sets: the problems you would most like a model to solve. They are your dreams for your work. Dream big.

When you get access to a new model, run the set on the first day. Compare each answer with the known answer, or check it the way you planned. Keep some money or spare usage ready for this test. When a dream is not realised with this model, keep it for the next one. Raise the complexity of the set step by step as each model solves more.

There is always a second route: break the problem down. With more time spent splitting a problem into smaller steps, each with a known answer, today's model may solve it. The other route is to wait for the next frontier model. Balance the effort now against the work later, by what is needed now.

The set shows you, on your own work, what the new model can now do. It also shows which checks you must still do by hand.
