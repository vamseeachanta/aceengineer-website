Why the model you use matters

In my experience, an older model often does not say "I cannot solve this". It gives an answer that looks correct. Our hypothesis: AI models improve in steps. A new model can solve problems that the model before it could not.

Frontier and behind

A frontier model is one of the most capable AI models available on a given date. A model one or two releases behind may cost less or respond faster for some workloads. Cost and speed depend on the models, workload and current pricing. Test whether the cheaper model meets your task's requirements, even for a summary, format change or first draft of a letter.

In my experience, the difference shows on problems where a common simplification is wrong, or where the problem is too large for the model. The model then often takes a shortcut and does not say so. Our hypothesis: on complex problems, improvements at each solution step can compound, raising the quality of the result and the chance of success. Better AI solutions also give people the opportunity to take further steps at the frontier.

A small gain can be a step change. Suppose many of your problems sit just beyond what a model two releases behind can do. Suppose, too, that a newer model scores only 1 to 2 % higher on public benchmarks (a hypothetical figure). It could then solve a whole group of your problems that the older model got wrong. For that group, the result is not 1 to 2 % better: it is a right answer in place of a wrong one. The schematic shows the idea; it is not a measurement.

A share of your problems, area by area

One way to think about a model: in each area of your work, it solves some share of your real problems correctly. Structural checks are one area. Report writing is another. A newer model can raise that share. Public league tables (benchmarks) test general problems, not yours. You can only find the share for your own work by testing the model on your own problems.

In my experience, our code once had to spell out the catenary equation for a hanging line. A frontier model now knows that equation without being given it. Our hypothesis: a newer model may also take on more engineering itself, as more mathematics and physics are trained into it. Engineering that once had to be written out for a model then becomes something it knows unaided. The illustration shows this direction in three stages. Where the engineer keeps the check, the work is checked against a known answer at every stage and then signed. A problem set with known answers, described below, is that check.

Note 1. Three frontier labs state that, depending on the user's settings, conversations in their consumer apps may be used to improve their models (OpenAI, Google and Anthropic; see Sources). Our hypothesis: frontier labs keep gaining user workload and research data, so each next model improves most where that work concentrates.

A worked example: a confident shortcut

This example is an illustration of the kind of error, not a test result. It is provided as a demonstration for this article.

The problem: find the first sloshing frequency of a rectangular tank, 1.0 m long, filled to 0.15 m.

The full linear-theory answer (small motions) includes the fill depth. For the first mode of a two-dimensional rectangular tank:

L = 1.0 m        tank length in the direction of sloshing
h = 0.15 m       fill depth
g = 9.81 m/s^2   gravity
k = pi / L       = 3.14 per m              (wave number of the first mode)
tanh(k h)        = tanh(0.471) = 0.439      (hyperbolic tangent; it tends to 1 for a deep fill)
w^2 = g k tanh(k h)  -> w = 3.68 rad/s     (w: angular frequency)
f = w / (2 pi)   = 0.586 Hz

A shortcut drops the tanh term. That form is for deep liquid:

w^2 = g k        -> w = 5.55 rad/s,  f = 0.884 Hz

The shortcut answer is about 51 % too high. It looks plausible: it has the right units, a clean formula and a confident tone. At a deep fill the two forms almost agree: at 0.70 m fill the difference is about 1 %. A check at a deep fill does not find the error. Only a check at the shallow fill shows it.

A frontier model can make the same mistake. Our hypothesis: it is less likely to do so, because of stronger mathematics and more due diligence on its own work.

Model, provider and harness: the paths a typical user takes

The same comparison applies to the provider. A model reaches you through a harness: the software around the model. The harness sets the model's instructions and the tools it may use, such as reading files, running code or searching. It also sets how much text the model keeps in view and how long it may work on a step. The same model can sit in a different harness at each provider.

Our hypothesis: on the same model, a weak harness can leave you the equivalent of two releases behind.

Match the model to the task ("horses for courses"). A small, fast, low-cost model can sometimes do the same job as a frontier model. Examples are the summary, format change etc. These examples should be determined based on work type and user experience.

Cost, speed and checking

A frontier model may cost more per task or run more slowly for a given workload. Compare the available models using that workload and the current pricing. Capability alone does not determine cost, speed or suitability. A frontier model is still not always right.

Check every answer that a decision depends on. Check the inputs and the units. Then check one case where you know the answer and where a shortcut would break: here, the shallow fill. If you know the full formula, or have the textbook open, the hand check above takes a few minutes.

Conclusion: cost and the long term

Treat your AI workspace like a group of employees you are training. In my experience, a frontier model returns fewer wrong answers to find and redo. Our hypothesis: a frontier model may cost a bit more per task; depending on the situation it costs more in the long run or pays back over the long term, and using a model that is behind compromises the training of your workspace. Your problem set shows whether it pays back for your work.

Keep your future problem sets

Keep your future problem sets: the problems you would most like a model to solve. They are your dreams for your work. Dream big.

When you get access to a new model, run the set on the first day. Compare each answer with the known answer, or check it the way you planned. Keep some money or spare usage ready for this test. When a dream is not realised with this model, keep it for the next one. Raise the complexity of the set step by step as each model solves more.

There is always a second route: break the problem down. With more time spent splitting a problem into smaller steps, each with a known answer, today's model may solve it. The other route is to wait for the next frontier model. Balance the effort now against the work later, by what is needed now.

The set shows you, on your own work, what the new model can now do. It also shows which checks you must still do by hand.

Appendix A. Reusable prompts

Prompt: "Here is my problem set, one problem per line, each with its known answer or the check I will apply. Solve each problem and state every assumption you make. Mark each problem you cannot solve, or solve only by a simplification, and name the simplification."

Run the same prompt and set on each new model and each provider you use. Compare the answers with the known answers. Data set: your own problem set; the worked example uses the textbook in Sources.
